During my biotech investing work, one framework that stayed with me was the **pharmaceutical value chain**. The model is simple on the surface, but it forces a more disciplined question than whether the science is interesting.

The better question is: **where exactly is value created, what risk is being removed at each step, and who is willing to pay for that de-risking?**

You can view the original framework here: [Pharmaceutical Value Chain Model]({{ '/assets/files/pharmaceutical-value-chain-model.pdf' | relative_url }}).

## Why this framework matters

Drug development is not a single binary bet. It is a sequence of handoffs across discovery, preclinical work, clinical development, regulatory execution, manufacturing, market access, and commercialization. At each stage, the asset becomes more valuable only if a specific uncertainty is reduced.

That means I tend to evaluate programs through three lenses:

1. **Scientific validity**: is the biological hypothesis strong enough to support a differentiated drug?
2. **Development feasibility**: can the team generate the evidence needed for the next value-inflection point?
3. **Commercial relevance**: if the program works, is the downstream market large, reachable, and strategically important?

This is why I do not think about drug development as good science versus bad science. I think about it as **evidence generation under capital constraints**.

## My working view of value creation

The most useful way to read the value chain is to map each stage to the dominant risk being removed.

- **Discovery** reduces target and mechanism risk.
- **Preclinical development** reduces translational and safety risk.
- **Clinical trials** reduce efficacy, dosing, and patient-selection risk.
- **Regulatory and CMC execution** reduce approval and supply risk.
- **Commercial launch** tests whether clinical value translates into adoption, reimbursement, and durable revenue.

Seen this way, a strong company is not just one with exciting biology. It is one that can remove the *right* uncertainty in the *right order*.

## What I pay attention to

When I think about drug development, I usually focus on the following questions:

- Is the program solving a real bottleneck in the value chain, or just adding technical complexity?
- Does the next experiment meaningfully increase the probability of success, or is it just incremental data collection?
- Is management building toward a credible partnering, financing, or approval milestone?
- Does the platform create repeatable leverage across multiple assets, or is it still a one-product story?

This is also why I am interested in enabling technologies. Better measurement tools, richer biological readouts, and more precise patient stratification can change the economics of the value chain by improving how quickly teams learn and where they can make better decisions.

## How this connects to my own work

My research in single-cell and spatial multi-omics is not directly a therapeutics program, but I think about it with the same logic. A technology is valuable if it improves decision quality somewhere along the development path.

For example, if a platform can reveal:

- which cell states respond to treatment,
- which protein interactions actually matter in tissue,
- which glycoform or immune signatures define a clinically relevant subgroup,

then it has potential value not just as an academic method, but as an engine for target discovery, biomarker strategy, translational profiling, and trial design.

That is the part of drug development I find most compelling: the intersection of mechanistic biology, measurement technology, and strategic decision-making.

## Bottom line

The pharmaceutical value chain is useful because it makes drug development legible. It breaks an extremely complex process into a series of value-transfer points, each tied to a specific risk, capability, and economic outcome.

That framing still shapes how I think about biotech opportunities today. The core issue is rarely just whether an idea is exciting. The harder and more important question is whether the science can be turned into a program that systematically earns its way to the next stage.
